In memory

Margaret Thompson Jensen

November 2, 1923  –  September 6, 2026

She was born in Peterson, a small village on the Root River, the fourth child of Sven, a machinist with a head for new ventures, and Agnes, a piano teacher. She lived to be 102. In between came a move to the Cities, a trailer with infant twins, a house on Clinton Avenue, seventy-eight years with Norb, and a church she called home.

1923 – 1941

Peterson

A small village on the Root River in Fillmore County, five miles upriver from Rushford.

Margaret was born at 1:45 in the morning on November 2, 1923, in the village of Peterson. The doctor came over from Rushford. On her birth certificate her name is spelled Margrette. She was the fourth living child of Sven and Agnes Thompson, after Paul, Dorothy and Lynn.

Her father gave his occupation that day as “Dealer in gravel.” By forty, Sven had already been a great many things: a homesteader raising flax in North Dakota, a chicken farmer whose pedigreed Wyandotte flock was wiped out by disease, a machinist trained at a gasoline-engine factory in Northfield, and for eight years Peterson’s car dealer. In 1922, with credit tight, he sold the dealership and put every dollar he had into gravel land and washing and screening equipment. Like many people, he wrote later, he didn’t believe the Depression would last. When it did, he took whatever work he could find: plumber, machinist, carpenter, common labor.

Before she was born: the motorcycle and the Metz

Ten years before Margaret arrived, her father ran the Peterson Machine Company. He sold motorcycles, and in January 1914 he threw a supper at the hotel for everyone who had bought one from him. The Rushford correspondent called it the first motorcycle club in the vicinity.

He was also courting Agnes Gullickson, who taught piano in Peterson and, as Sven wrote, “adored flowers and their culture.” He asked her to ride on the handlebars of his motorcycle. She flatly refused. He came back with a car.

“But, with persistence, finally she consented to a ride in a Metz roadster.”

Sven A. Thompson, To My Grandchildren, 1963

They married on March 30, 1915, her twenty-sixth birthday. Over the next eight years the dealership sold more than two hundred cars: Metz, Imperial, Dort, Ford, Buick.

A full house

When the census taker came to the Thompson house on April 22, 1930, Margaret was six. Her father was working as a plumber with a tin shop. The family owned their home, and they owned a radio. Living with them was Agnes’s younger sister, Cora Gullickson, who worked at the post office. After their mother died in 1911, Agnes had kept house for Cora and their brother Adolph; when Agnes married, Cora took over.

The next June, her little brother Stanley died. He was five, and Margaret was seven. He is buried at East Grace Cemetery in Peterson, where their parents were later buried too.

In March 1937, when Margaret was thirteen, the Root River flooded and jammed with ice at Peterson. The Bue Studio of Lanesboro photographed it.

By April 1940 Margaret was sixteen and in her third year of high school. Her father now had his own plumbing shop. Paul was away at college. Lynn, twenty, was listed as a “new worker” who had been looking for a job for twenty-two weeks, a sign of how slowly the Depression lifted in small towns.

She graduated from Peterson High School in 1941.

1941 – 1948

To the Cities

A war plant, a new city, and a young sailor from Worthington.

In 1941 her father hired on with the U.S. government as a machinist at the new cartridge plant near New Brighton, the Twin Cities Ordnance Plant, which was being built to turn out rifle and machine-gun ammunition. The family moved to St. Paul. Margaret was seventeen.

Her senior portrait is signed by the Zintsmaster Studio, which in 1941 had rooms in the Albrecht Building at 816 Nicollet Avenue in downtown Minneapolis. She may have sat for it just as the family was moving.

Norbert Vernon Jensen was born on June 1, 1925, in Worthington, in the southwest corner of the state, the son of Raymond and Gertrude (Fischer) Jensen. He served in naval aviation during the Second World War.

Still missing from the record: where Margaret worked in the war years, and how she and Norb met. If you know the story, this is where it belongs.

1948 – 1953

Trailer No. 3

Married in St. Paul, and twins before the second anniversary.

Margaret and Norb married in St. Paul on August 28, 1948. She was twenty-four, and he was twenty-three.

Their twin sons, Mark and Keith, were born in Worthington on January 22, 1950. Three months later the census found the four of them in trailer #3 at the Red Top Cabins trailer camp, 7421 Lyndale Avenue South, in Richfield. The camp was also a tourist court. Their neighbors included an airline pilot and a pipeline foreman.

Norb was twenty-four. He had come from Nobles County the year before, had worked twenty-six weeks in 1949, and had been going to school since February. Margaret was twenty-six and keeping house, with two infants in a trailer.

In St. Paul that April, her father, sixty-six, was still working as a machinist.

Kristen was born on September 4, 1951, in Minneapolis.

1953 – 1970s

4832 Clinton Avenue

A house of their own in south Minneapolis, year after year in the city directory.

By 1953 the Jensens owned 4832 Clinton Avenue in south Minneapolis, and they had a telephone. The city directory lists the household year after year under Norb’s name, with Margaret in parentheses, usually as “Margt T.” The T is for Thompson. The listings also trace Norb’s work: welder, carpenter, plant superintendent, and carpenter for the city.

How to read a directory line: h means householder (at home), Margt is Margaret, wldr welder, carp carpenter, plant supt plant superintendent. A few years misprint her initial.

Bruce, the youngest, was born on October 26, 1956.

On February 7, 1959, Margaret’s mother died suddenly in St. Paul. Her father came to live on Clinton Avenue. Four years later, in February 1963, Sven sat down and wrote the story of his life and Agnes’s for his grandchildren, which included Margaret’s four. He titled it To My Grandchildren.

“After her death, I lived with my youngest daughter, Margaret, and her husband, Norbert V. Jensen, in Minneapolis, Minn. for a while.”

Sven A. Thompson, 1963 · read the whole letter

Sven died in May 1965 in Minneapolis, at eighty-one. He was buried at East Grace Cemetery in Peterson, where Agnes and Stanley lie.

The Clinton Avenue listing stayed in the phone book through at least 1975.

Faith

First Free

First Evangelical Free Church, 5150 Chicago Avenue South, Minneapolis.

Margaret was born into a household that had just made a promise. In the last lines of his letter, her father wrote:

“During 1923, Agnes and I agreed to rededicate our lives to our dear Savior and Lord, Jesus Christ. May this resolve also be shared by all our kin and generations, now and for the years to come.”

Sven A. Thompson, February 1963

Her mother had played organ for church choirs. In Minneapolis, Margaret and Norb made First Free their home church. Their faith was very important to both of them, the family says, and it became home to the next generations too.

“I have called First Evangelical Free Church my home church for 30 years. Growing up close by in Richfield, I attended with my parents and grandparents (Norb & Margaret Jensen) until my family volunteered for the Brookview church plant in 1987. Six years later, after Brookview closed down, I elected to return to First Free. This place is truly dear to me. FEFC is where I grew up and was baptized.”

Peter McWilliams, her grandson, Church Family News, October 2016

2007 – 2026

A Century

Seventy, seventy-two, seventy-five years married, and a hundredth birthday.

When her sister Dorothy died in 2007, the obituary listed “a sister: Margaret (and Norbert) Jensen of Bloomington.” Margaret was the last of the five Thompson children.

First Free kept count of the anniversaries in its newsletter. In October 2018: “Happy anniversary to Norbert & Margaret Jensen, who celebrated 70 years of marriage in August.” In 2020 they were on the list of seniors married sixty-five years or more: “Aug. 28 | 72 years.” In August 2023: “Happy 75th wedding anniversary to Margaret and Norbert Jensen!”

On October 30, 2022, three days before her ninety-ninth birthday, the family threw her a party, and she wore a gold tiara that read “Aged to Perfection.” Norb and all four children were there: the same six who had sat for a studio portrait some fifty-five years before.

That December the newsletter added one more line: “Happy Birthday to Margaret Jensen who celebrated her 100th birthday last month!”

On June 8, 2025, the family gathered for Norb’s birthday. He had turned one hundred the week before, and he wore his Honor Flight shirt.

Margaret died in Bloomington on September 6, 2026. She was 102. She and Norb had been married for seventy-eight years.

February 1963

To My Grandchildren

Margaret’s father was seventy-nine and living in her house when he wrote down his life and her mother’s for the next generation. The letter survives in the family tree, and it is the reason we know about the motorcycle, the Metz roadster and the gravel pit.

“She used her easy-going personality to smooth over the rough spots, to make life wonderful and worth while for the whole family.”

Sven, on Agnes

Where the records disagree
  • Sven’s death: the family tree says 25 May 1965 in St. Paul; the state death index says 18 May 1965 in Hennepin County. This page says May 1965.
  • Agnes and St. Olaf: Sven wrote that she graduated from the music department on 7 June 1909. St. Olaf’s printed register of music graduates for 1906–1919 does not list her. She may have earned the teacher’s certificate he also mentions. The page tells it as Sven told it.
  • The carburetor: family lore says Sven sent carburetor plans to Henry Ford. No patent in his name has been found.
  • Spellings: “Margrette” on her birth certificate; “Kieth” on the 1950 census; “Swen” on the 1930 and 1940 censuses.
